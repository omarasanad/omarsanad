# Summer heat & digestive health: medical fact-check report

**For:** Dr. Abubakr Ahmed Sanad. His clinical judgement overrides anything below.
**Prepared:** 8 Nov 2026 · **Status:** draft for review.
**Arabic version:** `01_fact_check_report_ar.md`.

## How this was checked, and its limits

PubMed, PMC, WHO, AGA and doi.org are blocked by this production environment's network policy. Claims were checked using:
- primary studies and systematic reviews, retrieved through a research-paper search engine (abstracts read);
- guideline and agency texts found by web search, mostly via secondary copies or summaries;
- established physiology.

Where only a secondary source was available, the table says so (**"verify"**). Those points should be confirmed against the original before publishing.

**The key distinction.** The original script mixes three very different situations:
- **ordinary summer weather** at rest;
- **severe passive heat stress**, where core body temperature itself rises;
- **strenuous or prolonged exercise in the heat.**

Most of the dramatic findings (gut barrier injury, endotoxin leak, 80% falls in gut blood flow) come from the last two, mainly from athletes and soldiers. The corrected script keeps the mechanisms but says plainly when they apply.

## Detail on each claim

### 1. Does heat send ~70% of the blood to the skin?

- **Physiology:** at rest in comfortable temperatures, skin blood flow is ~0.25–0.5 L/min.
- **Under pronounced passive heat stress** (core temperature rising):
  - skin blood flow can reach **≈7–8 L/min**;
  - cardiac output rises to **≈13 L/min**;
  - so roughly **half** of cardiac output goes to the skin.
  - The rise in skin flow is partly met by the higher cardiac output and partly by *redistribution* away from the splanchnic (gut), renal and muscle beds (Crandall & González-Alonso 2010; Rowell 1974).
- **Conclusion:** "70%" overstates even the extreme case, and the extreme case is not an ordinary summer day.
- **On screen:** "حتى حوالي ٥٠٪ من ضخ القلب · في الإجهاد الحراري الشديد" (up to about 50% of cardiac output, in severe heat stress), with the source in small type. **Narration:** no number.

### 2. Heat stress and gut blood flow and function

- **Passive heat stress:** gut vascular conductance falls and blood volume shifts out of the splanchnic bed (Crandall & González-Alonso 2010).
  - *Verify:* the exact percentage drop for passive heating; I could not open the original Rowell data.
- **Exercise:**
  - Gut blood flow falls in proportion to exercise intensity; with long or intense exercise it **may fall by 80% or more** (ter Steege & Kolkman 2012 review).
  - In healthy men cycling for 60 min, falling gut perfusion led to measurable small-intestinal injury and transiently increased permeability (van Wijck 2011).
- **Function:**
  - Strenuous exercise impairs gastric emptying, slows small-bowel transit and causes malabsorption, and **heat stress makes these worse**.
  - Significant disturbance appears from about **≥2 h at 60% VO₂max** (Costa 2017 systematic review).
  - GI symptoms (nausea, cramps, diarrhoea, bloating) affect ~30–90% of endurance athletes depending on conditions.
- **Not supported:** "temporary paralysis", or ulcers and gastritis getting "much worse" from summer weather.
- **Kept:** heat plus exertion → slower stomach emptying → heaviness and nausea.

### 3. Gut barrier and endotoxins

**Human evidence:**
- **Pires et al. 2017** (systematic review, 16 studies): exercise-induced rises in core temperature correlated strongly with rises in intestinal permeability (r = 0.79), and **>39 °C was always associated with increased permeability**.
- **Snipe et al. 2017:** 2 h of running at 35 °C vs 22 °C greatly increased gut-cell injury markers (I-FABP +432% vs +127%), inflammatory cytokines, and GI symptoms (incidence 90% vs 70%).
- **Alhadad et al. 2026:** at the same peak core temperature (~39.4 °C), running injured the gut lining while passive hot-water immersion did not. The **combination of heat with exertion** is what matters.

**Clinical relevance:** endotoxin translocation through a hyperthermia-damaged gut wall is thought to aggravate **heat stroke** (Patel 2023).

**Animal and cell studies** describe the tight-junction mechanism (Lian 2020 review). That supports the "gaps between cells widen" visual, which is labelled as severe heat stress in the reel.

**Not supported:**
- "Leaky gut syndrome" as a diagnosis.
- Leaky gut as the cause of ordinary summer tiredness. Common explanations are dehydration, heat itself, and poor sleep.

### 4. Heat and the gut microbiome

- **Animal evidence:**
  - Heat exposure in mice and livestock consistently shifts the gut microbiota and reduces diversity.
  - Faecal transplant experiments in rodents suggest dysbiosis can amplify heat injury (Zhang 2026 rodent synthesis; Huus & Ley 2021 review).
- **Human evidence:**
  - The gut microbiota shows **seasonal variation**, driven largely by changes in diet, activity and environment (Cocomazzi 2025).
  - No human study shows that summer heat causes persistent bloating "even with a 100% healthy diet".
- **More likely causes of summer bloating:**
  - carbonated drinks (gas);
  - eating fast (swallowed air);
  - large meals;
  - **watermelon**, which is classified as **high-FODMAP** (excess fructose, fructans, polyols) in standard serving sizes and can cause bloating in sensitive people, e.g. those with IBS. *Verify the serving thresholds in the Monash FODMAP app.*

### 5. Dehydration and stomach acid

- **No evidence** that dehydration raises gastric acid concentration in a clinically meaningful way.
- Water transiently raises intragastric pH above 4 in most people for ~3 minutes (Karamanolis 2008). Acid output itself is hormonally and neurally regulated.
- **Established effects of dehydration:** thirst, headache, dizziness, nausea (part of heat exhaustion), and constipation with low fluid intake.

### 6. Carbonated drinks, coffee and reflux

- **ACG 2022 GERD guideline:**
  - avoid meals within 2–3 h of bedtime;
  - elevate the head of the bed for night-time symptoms;
  - weight loss if overweight;
  - stop smoking;
  - **suggests** avoiding trigger foods (weak recommendation). Triggers are individual: coffee, carbonated drinks, fatty food, chocolate, spicy and acidic foods are common culprits for *some* patients.
- **The script now says:** "in many people", with no "fierce attacks", and no claim of gastritis caused by summer.

### 7. Warning signs

- **ACG/CAG 2017 dyspepsia guideline:**
  - endoscopy for **age ≥60** with dyspepsia;
  - under 60, alarm features prompt case-by-case endoscopy. They include **unintentional weight loss**, **progressive dysphagia**, odynophagia, **iron-deficiency anaemia**, **persistent vomiting**, an abdominal mass, a family history of upper-GI cancer, and **GI bleeding** (bleeding is in the VBAD mnemonic; *verify* it in the guideline text).
- **Pain that wakes the patient** is a long-standing clinical red flag (e.g. peptic ulcer) and is kept as the doctor's clinical judgement.
- **Heat illness:**
  - heat exhaustion: nausea or vomiting, headache, dizziness, heavy sweating, weakness;
  - heat stroke: confusion, slurred speech, fainting, very high temperature. Cool the person immediately and **treat as an emergency** (CDC guidance via secondary sources; *verify* on the CDC page).

### 8. Food safety in hot weather (added)

- **Temperature and food poisoning:** in 10 European countries, salmonellosis rose roughly linearly with temperature above a threshold of ~6 °C, strongest at a 1-week lag (Kovats 2004).
- **Refrigeration rule:** FDA/CDC consumer guidance is to refrigerate perishable or cooked food **within 2 hours**, or **within 1 hour when it is 32 °C (90 °F) or hotter**. WHO's "Five Keys to Safer Food" includes "keep food at safe temperatures".
